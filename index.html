What the technology does

Personalisation is a supply chain, not a feature

Recommendation engines are standard practice in tourism and retail. Collaborative filtering matches a traveller against millions of earlier bookings, then enrichment services attach browsing history, review sentiment and social data to sharpen the prediction. Relevance raises conversion; conversion pays for the pipeline.

The ethical risk is equally simple. Every enrichment step adds a party holding customer information, and a new point where consent, access control and retention can fail (Shackel & Smith, 2025). Exposed data is a target, not an accident: 69% of 2024 breach notifications came from malicious or criminal attacks (OAIC, 2025b).

When an engine is treated as a product to buy rather than a process to govern, nobody owns its failures.

The dilemma

A finding nobody senior wants to hear

Customers who booked through the engine began reporting phishing emails, texts and bank fraud matching details they had entered. I raised it. Head office was not interested, and I could not tell customers to stop using the product.

The decision was not whether to feel uneasy. It was what to do with a finding the people with authority had already dismissed.

Three details make this a dilemma rather than a complaint. The harm is invisible until it reaches a customer. The evidence sits in systems I can read but not control. And the person expected to act holds less power than those who caused the exposure.

Ethics rarely collapses because someone chooses evil. It collapses because responsibility is spread wider than accountability.

Ethical considerations

Four obligations, none of them optional

Integrity and objectivity
A finding cannot be softened because a superior finds it inconvenient. Deference is not neutrality.
APES 110, ss. 110.1–110.2
Confidentiality
Customer data cannot be disclosed again, which rules out investigating by exporting the dataset to personal tools.
APES 110, s. 140.1
Privacy and security duties
Reasonable technical and organisational steps must secure personal information, and a suspected eligible breach assessed within 30 days.
Privacy Act 1988 (Cth), sch 1 cl 11; pt IIIC
Professional behaviour
Reputational and public-interest costs belong inside the decision, not to a media cycle three weeks later.
APES 110, s. 110.5

Deconstructing it

Computational thinking, four moves

The brief pairs computational thinking with human judgement: the method locates the problem, judgement decides the response. Open each step.

1Decomposition
Split the event into four questions: what data flowed in, who received it, which control failed, and who owns that control. Ambiguity survives only while the problem stays whole.
2Pattern recognition
Breaches repeat a few patterns: over-broad access, third-party enrichment, no retention limit, no audit trail. The national data shows the same causes recurring across sectors (OAIC, 2025b).
3Abstraction
Strip out the features until one rule remains: a customer's data may only reach a party that has agreed, in writing, to protect it. The algorithm, the dashboard, the campaign — all detail.
4Algorithm design
Turn the rule into a sequence any employee can run without permission: verify, log, escalate, notify, remediate. A response that depends on one person's courage is not a control; it is a hope.

How I would circumvent it

Five moves, in this order

1

Verify before you accuse

Reproduce the data path, take a sample I am authorised to hold, and record dates, systems and volumes. A dated log is harder to dismiss than a concern.

2

Put it in writing

Escalate to risk and compliance, copy the audit committee, and ask for a decision in writing. A written refusal makes the risk an institutional fact someone must own.

3

Meet the legal clock

Treat suspicion as the trigger: assess within 30 days and, if eligible, notify the regulator and affected customers (Privacy Act 1988 (Cth), pt IIIC). Notification is a duty, not a public-relations choice.

4

Contain, then tell the truth

Revoke excess permissions, end enrichment with vendors that cannot evidence security, and tell affected customers what happened and what changed. Silence protects the company for a week, then damages it for a decade (Labrecque et al., 2021).

5

Redesign, and escalate if blocked

Add consent, data minimisation, retention limits and human review of high-risk recommendations. If leadership still refuses, make a protected disclosure under the Corporations Act 2001 (Cth), pt 9.4AAA.
